Cover Note to Spine / Orthopedic Provider
Re: Reasonable Accommodation Medical Documentation for [EMPLOYEE]
Private and Confidential — For Provider Use Only

Dear [Provider Name],
Thank you for your willingness to provide medical documentation in support of my reasonable
accommodation request with the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services. I have prepared
a draft letter to assist you in responding to the agency's documentation requirements. Before you
review that draft, I want to share some context that explains why certain elements are framed the
way they are.
Please read this note alongside the draft letter. You are not obligated to use my language, and you
should revise any statement that does not reflect your independent clinical judgment. The goal of
this note is to help you understand the legal and strategic context so that your letter is as effective
as possible.
My letter to you will be submitted alongside a coordinated letter from my neurology and
psychiatry provider. The two letters address different clinical aspects of my condition and are
designed to be read together as a complete submission. Your letter focuses on musculoskeletal
functional limitations. You do not need to address neurological or cognitive limitations, as those
are covered by the other provider.

1. Why the Letter Does Not Include Diagnosis Information
The HHS documentation guidance explicitly states that no diagnosis information is required for
the narrative questions. We have deliberately avoided including specific diagnoses in the draft for
two reasons.
First, the legal standard for reasonable accommodation focuses on functional limitations, not
diagnoses. What the agency is required to assess is what your patient cannot do or has difficulty
doing, and how an accommodation addresses that. A diagnosis alone does not establish this
connection.
Second, limiting the letter to functional and impairment-based language protects your patient's
medical privacy to the greatest extent possible while still providing the agency with everything it
is legally entitled to receive.
If you believe a diagnosis is clinically necessary to explain a particular limitation, you may include
it. However, we ask that you consider whether the functional description alone is sufficient before

doing so.

2. Why the Letter Recommends a Permanent Accommodation
The draft letter specifically recommends that accommodations be granted on a permanent and
indefinite basis. This is intentional and important.
[If applicable:] In a prior proceeding, my medical documentation recommended a [DURATION] accommodation.
The agency used the time-limited nature of that recommendation to treat the accommodation as
temporary and subject to expiration. A permanent recommendation prevents that outcome and is
clinically appropriate if my underlying musculoskeletal impairment is not expected to resolve.
If you are comfortable attesting that my impairment is chronic and permanent with no anticipated
recovery, I ask that you say so explicitly and state that you do not recommend a time-limited
accommodation. If the condition is more variable and a permanent recommendation is not
clinically supportable, please discuss this with me before finalizing the letter so I can adjust my
approach.

3. Why the Letter Recommends Telework Specifically
The draft answers the question of why your patient requires a specific accommodation rather than
why she requires any accommodation. This is deliberate.
In a prior proceeding, the agency interpreted a general statement of accommodation need as
authorization to offer whatever it chose, including accommodations that did not address my
primary functional barriers. By specifically recommending full-time telework and explaining the
clinical reasons why it is the appropriate accommodation, your letter limits the agency's ability to
substitute a less effective alternative and call it sufficient.
From a musculoskeletal standpoint, I am asking you to recommend telework specifically because
my primary barrier is not desk work itself, but the fixed-posture transit demands associated with
reporting to the office. An ergonomic workstation addresses pain at the desk. It does not address
the pain that accumulates before I reach my desk. Telework eliminates that daily pain
accumulation by removing the transit requirement entirely.

4. Why the Letter Addresses Alternative Accommodations
The agency's documentation form asks whether alternative accommodations would meet your
patient's needs. We have drafted a response that acknowledges ergonomic workstation
modifications as a partial measure while explaining why they are clinically insufficient compared
to full-time telework.

This framing is important. If the letter does not address alternatives, the agency may fill that
silence by offering an ergonomic chair and a sit-stand desk and claiming those are adequate. By
addressing the question directly and explaining the clinical limitations of onsite alternatives, your
letter makes it significantly harder for the agency to deny telework and substitute a lesser
accommodation.
I am not asking you to say that no alternatives exist. I am asking you to say, if it is clinically
accurate, that onsite ergonomic modifications are less effective than telework for my specific
musculoskeletal limitations because they do not address the transit-related pain accumulation
that is my primary barrier. Please review the draft language and confirm it reflects your clinical
judgment.

5. Why the Letter Frames the Office Worksite as an Accessibility Barrier
The draft frames the office worksite itself as functionally inaccessible due to transit-related
postural demands, rather than framing the issue as a request to accommodate your patient's
commute. This distinction is legally significant.
The agency has previously denied accommodation requests on the basis that it is not obligated to
accommodate an employee's commute. While that position is legally questionable, I want to avoid
giving the agency any basis to rely on it here. The relevant barrier is not the transportation itself. It
is the musculoskeletal consequence of the fixed-posture seated positioning required to reach the
office, which depletes my functional capacity before my workday begins. Telework addresses this
by modifying the worksite itself, not by changing how I travel.
I ask that when describing the transit-related demands in your letter, you focus on what those
demands do to me musculoskeletally, rather than on the transportation itself. For example,
describing "fixed-posture seated positioning required to reach the office worksite" is preferable to
describing "the commute."

6. The Travel Modification Requests
Your letter also supports the travel modification requests, specifically upgraded seating class,
direct flights, and a travel day distinct from the workday. These are recognized accommodations
for postural tolerance limitations and are appropriate when standard travel conditions cannot
accommodate your patient's functional needs.
If you are asked whether first or business class seating is medically necessary, the clinical basis is
that standard aircraft seating does not allow sufficient postural adjustment or movement for a
patient with my musculoskeletal limitations, and that the extended fixed-posture demands of
standard seating are associated with acute exacerbations. Please include this reasoning in your
letter if you can support it clinically.
